Krishna Janmashtami

When is it Celebrated?

Krishna Janmashtami is observed on the Ashtami (8th day) of the Krishna Paksha (waning phase of the moon) in the Hindu month of Bhadrapada. It usually falls in August or September each year.

In 2026, Krishna Janmashtami will be celebrated on Saturday, September 5 (dates may vary based on regional lunar calendars).

Why is it Celebrated?

Krishna Janmashtami marks the birth anniversary of Lord Krishna, the eighth avatar of Vishnu.

Lord Krishna is revered as the divine teacher of:

  • Love
  • Righteousness (Dharma)
  • Devotion

His life and teachings are preserved in sacred scriptures such as the Bhagavad Gita and the Mahabharata.

Devotees celebrate this day to honor Krishna’s miraculous birth in a prison cell in Mathura, where he incarnated to restore dharma and guide humanity.

His life symbolizes:

  • Victory of good over evil
  • Divine love and compassion
  • Spiritual wisdom and guidance

How is Janmashtami Celebrated?

Midnight Darshan
Lord Krishna is believed to have been born at midnight. Devotees stay awake through the night and gather in temples for special prayers, bhajans, and chanting until the sacred birth moment.
Fasting and Bhajans
Many devotees observe a fast until midnight. Throughout the day, homes and temples echo with devotional songs, prayers, and chanting of Krishna’s holy names.
Temple Decorations and Jhula Seva
Temples and homes are decorated with flowers and lights. Baby Krishna (Bal Gopal) is placed in a beautifully decorated cradle (jhula), and devotees gently rock it while singing devotional hymns.
Welcoming Krishna with Footprints
Devotees draw tiny footprints from the entrance of their homes to the prayer room, symbolizing the arrival of baby Krishna and inviting divine blessings.
Offerings and Prasadam
Special sweets and vegetarian dishes are lovingly prepared and offered to Krishna. Butter and milk-based sweets are especially popular, reflecting Krishna’s fondness for dairy.
Cultural Programs
Communities organize devotional dramas and dance performances depicting Krishna’s divine life and playful leelas, sharing spiritual stories through art and celebration.

Holi – Festival of Colors

When is Holi Celebrated?

Holi is celebrated on the full moon day (Purnima) of the Hindu month of Phalguna, which usually falls in February or March.

The celebration lasts for two days:

  • Day 1 — Holika Dahan: Bonfire night symbolizing the burning of evil
  • Day 2 — Rangwali Holi: The grand celebration with colors, water, and festivities

Why is Holi Celebrated?

Victory of Good over Evil

Holi commemorates the story of Prahlada, a devoted follower of Vishnu. The demon king Hiranyakashipu tried to kill him for his devotion. His sister Holika sat in a fire with Prahlada, believing she was immune to flames. But divine grace saved Prahlada while Holika burned — symbolizing that faith and goodness always triumph over evil.

Celebration of Divine Love

Holi also celebrates the playful love of Krishna and Radha. In Vrindavan and Mathura, Holi is celebrated with great devotion, remembering Krishna playfully applying colors on Radha and the gopis.

How is Holi Celebrated?

Holika Dahan (Bonfire Night)
Families gather in the evening to light bonfires, symbolizing the victory of good over evil, inspired by the story of Prahlada and Holika.
Devotees offer prayers for protection, health, and prosperity, while the fire represents purification and new beginnings.
Playing with Colors
On Rangwali Holi, people joyfully smear colored powders (gulal) and splash water using balloons and pichkaris.
The streets come alive with laughter, music, and dance, breaking social barriers and spreading happiness among all.
Festive Celebrations
Communities organize devotional songs, folk dances, and cultural programs depicting Krishna and Radha’s playful leelas.
These gatherings strengthen family and community bonds while celebrating life’s vibrancy.
Sweets and Festive Foods
Special sweets like gujiya, malpua, and drinks like thandai are prepared and shared.
Sharing festive foods symbolizes love, togetherness, and joy among family, friends, and neighbors.
Spreading Joy and Harmony
Holi encourages forgiveness, reconciliation, and the renewal of relationships.
The festival promotes friendship, unity, and harmony, making it a celebration of life and human connection.

Govardana Puja – Celebrating the Lifting of Govardhan Hill

When is it Celebrated?
Govardana Puja is observed on the fourth day of Diwali (the day after Naraka Chaturdashi), in the Hindu month of Kartik (October–November). It commemorates the day when Lord Krishna lifted the Govardhan Hill to protect the villagers of Vrindavan from torrential rains sent by Indra.
Why is it Celebrated?
The festival honors Krishna’s divine protection of his devotees and expresses gratitude for nature’s bounty—the hill, cows, and fertile land that sustain life.
It also reinforces the lesson of humility and devotion, as the villagers realized that faith in Krishna is greater than worship of material power.
How is Govardana Puja Celebrated?
Creating the Govardhan Hill Replica: Devotees build a small mound of cow dung or soil, decorated with flowers, grains, and miniature cows, representing Govardhan Hill. Offerings of fruits, vegetables, sweets, and milk products are placed around it.
Worship and Prayers: Devotees perform aarti around the hill replica and sing bhajans and kirtans dedicated to Krishna, invoking his blessings.
Annadanam / Food Offerings: Vegetarian dishes, especially milk-based sweets and satvik foods, are offered to Krishna and then shared among family and community members.
Community Participation: Temples and homes host gatherings where people share prasad, recount the story of Krishna lifting the hill, and children play with miniature cow and hill models, symbolizing Krishna’s divine leela.
Symbolic Practices: Many devotees worship cows, linking the day to Krishna’s love for cows and pastoral life. Some communities perform processions around the Govardhan replica, chanting Krishna’s holy names.
